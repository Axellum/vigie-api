# Solar eclipses: Vigie's computation against NASA's tables (2 August 2027)

*A check made on 28 October 2026 with [`tools/check_eclipses.py`](../../tools/check_eclipses.py) (`--markdown`, NASA's path page saved the same
day). Nothing in it is an estimate: the method is the textbook one, and this page shows that the code applies it as NASA does.*

## 1. Conclusion

From the same Besselian elements as NASA's, Vigie finds **the same eclipse to a fraction of a second and a fraction of a kilometre** on the
101 rows of NASA's path table of the total eclipse of 2 August 2027: the instant of the maximum on the central line within 0.19 s of the
row's time, the central duration within 0.18 s, the limits of the path within 0.12 km. The place of greatest eclipse (25°30.3'N,
33°11.0'E) gets its maximum at 10:06:37.8 UT (NASA: 10:06:37.7) and 6 min 22.5 s of totality (NASA: 6 min 22.6 s).

Against **astronomy-engine** (MIT; its own ephemerides of the Sun and the Moon and its own delta-T), at eight places from Cádiz to Jeddah,
the kind of eclipse is the same everywhere, the share of the Sun hidden agrees to 0.001, and the contacts differ by 4 to 8 seconds, all in
the same direction. That is the difference of delta-T: astronomy-engine extrapolates 76.0 s for 2027 (its `DeltaT_EspenakMeeus`, read
with the installed version), NASA's page says 71.7 s. Which one is right will be known only in 2027; NASA's is served.

## 2. Method

The bundled file (`app/data/solar-eclipses.json.gz`, `tools/build_eclipses.py`) holds, for each of the 224 eclipses of 2001 to 2100, the
polynomial Besselian elements of its NASA page. From them, as the Explanatory Supplement to the Astronomical Almanac (1992, chapter 8) and
Meeus (Elements of Solar Eclipses 1951-2200) write it:

- a place on the WGS84 ellipsoid at sea level is put in the fundamental plane (its `xi`, `eta`, `zeta`);
- the maximum is the instant the shadow axis passes nearest (Newton's method); the contacts are the instants the distance to the axis equals
  the radius of the penumbra (C1, C4) or of the umbra (C2, C3), found by bisection;
- the magnitude and the share of the Sun hidden come from the two radii and that distance; the Sun's altitude from the place's hour angle;
- the central line is where the axis meets the ellipsoid; the path is the union of the umbra's footprints every 20 seconds.

As in NASA's tables: no refraction, no height above sea level, a spherical Moon (NASA writes that its limb changes the durations by 1 to 3
seconds and the limits by 1 to 3 km). A contact outside the six hours over which the elements are valid is left out; a place where the
Sun stays down from the first contact to the last sees nothing.

## 4. Limits

- **Delta-T.** For a future eclipse it is NASA's extrapolation. If the Earth's rotation drifts otherwise, every time moves by the
  difference (a few seconds by 2027), and the path moves by about 0.4 km in longitude per second.
- **The Moon's limb.** Its valleys and mountains shift the contacts at the edge of the path the most; the path tables of NASA have the
  same limit. Close to a limit, a few hundred metres decide whether a place sees totality.
- **The bands of the map** come from a 0.5-degree grid: their edges are good to about 50 km, which is plenty for the share hidden but not
  for a limit of the path (the path itself is drawn from the footprints, to under a kilometre).
- Only one eclipse's table was compared row by row; the code is the same for every eclipse, and the elements of each are its NASA page's.
