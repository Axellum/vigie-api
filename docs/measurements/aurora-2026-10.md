# The aurora estimate on past nights: does it tell who saw one?

*A measure made on 28 October 2026. The tool is [`tools/measure_aurora.py`](../../tools/measure_aurora.py); every figure below is written
by it (`score --markdown`) into [`aurora-2026-10-results.json`](aurora-2026-10-results.json). The method and its criterion are
[ADR 0012](../decisions/0012-aurora-estimate.md), committed before the test set was scored (commit `5454fa9`).*

## 1. Conclusion

**The criterion of ADR 0012 holds on all four points**, so the API serves the estimate, marked as Vigie's own:

1. Ranking: the area under the ROC curve is **0.785** on the 15,573 reports of 2023 to 2025 (0.750 in Europe alone): a report that saw an
   aurora gets a higher chance than one that did not about four times in five.
2. Calibration: the share of reports that saw an aurora grows with the chance (35 %, 45 %, 63 %, 87 % over the four bins).
3. The big nights: of the reports that saw an aurora in the dark on **10-11 May 2024**, 98 % (world) and 96 % (Europe) got "possible" or
   better; on **10-11 October 2024**, 94 % and 91 %.
4. It beats the simplest rule drawn from SWPC's own scale (mean log-likelihood -0.646 against -1.659).

**What it does not do well**, which the answers and the site must not hide:

- **"No" is not a promise.** Of the reports the estimate put under 5 %, **35 % saw an aurora** (19 % in the years it was fitted on). The
  reports come from people who went out to look, mostly on nights of storms, often with a phone camera that sees more than the eye: the
  share is inflated, but the estimate is too pessimistic at its low end. The site says "very unlikely", not "none".
- **False alarms are common.** Half the reports that saw nothing (54 %) got 20 % or more; among those who said the sky was clear (the
  form asks it only of those who saw nothing), 58 %. The clouds are not the explanation.
- **Within one night it ranks places poorly.** On a given night the area under the curve falls to 0.42 to 0.79: the estimate knows the
  night well (through the Kp) and the place less well. The Kp is one number for the planet every three hours; the real oval bulges and moves.
- **The Moon factor does not help.** Without it the ranking is the same (0.786) and the log-likelihood better (-0.629). It is kept as
  committed (changing it after the test would be fitting on the test); ADR 0012 lists it as an open point.
- **Kp alone is better calibrated** (log-likelihood -0.554) though worse at ranking (0.737): the estimate is too sure of itself at both ends.
- **What was not measured**: the cloud factor (a report does not say how cloudy it was, except "clear" when nothing was seen), SWPC's
  forecast Kp (the Kp here is the one GFZ published afterwards: a perfect forecast), and the OVATION oval (SWPC keeps no archive of it).

## 2. Method

**The reference.** "Aurorasaurus Web Observations (2014-2025)" (MacDonald et al. 2026, <https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.16783265>, CC BY-NC
4.0), the cleaned file: 22,280 reports of people who looked at the sky, each with a place, a time span and `see_aurora` true (16,368) or false
(5,912). 22,016 are used: 264 have no usable time span (or one over 6 hours), position, answer, or Kp. The time of a report is the middle of
its span.

**The inputs**, as the API computes them: the Kp of the report's 3-hour interval from GFZ Potsdam (`Kp_ap_since_1932.txt`, Matzka et al. 2021,
<https://doi.org/10.5880/Kp.0001>, CC BY 4.0); the magnetic latitude from the bundled IGRF-14 apex grid (epoch 2026.5 for every report: the
field moves by a fraction of a degree over these years); the Sun and the Moon by `astronomy-engine` at the report's place and time. No cloud
factor.

**The split.** `fit` read only the 6,443 reports made before 2023-01-01 and chose the two settings of the edge with the best mean
log-likelihood on a grid (55 to 75 degrees at Kp 0, 0.5 to 5 degrees a Kp unit): **62.5 and 1.9**. The ADR, those settings and the
criterion were committed (`5454fa9`) before `score` read the 15,573 reports from 2023-01-01. A first fit with a centred dipole as the
magnetic latitude was dropped before any score, when the dipole was found to put France 7 degrees and Spain 10 degrees too far north
(ADR 0012).

**The scores.** The area under the ROC curve (ties counted half); the mean Bernoulli log-likelihood with each chance kept within
[0.02, 0.98] (the estimate says 0 and 1); the Brier score; the share of the reports that saw an aurora by bin of chance.

Criterion of ADR 0012:

- 1, area under the curve at least 0.75: **holds** (0.785)
- 2, calibration grows from bin to bin: **holds** (0.353, 0.446, 0.626, 0.869)
- 3, the big nights, seen in the dark, at least 70 % "possible": **holds** (97.6 %, 96.4 %, 93.8 %, 91.4 %)
- 4, better than the G-scale line: **holds** (-0.646 against -1.659)
- Overall: **holds**

## 4. Limits of the measure

- The reports are not a random sample: people report when they look, and look when an aurora is announced. The shares "seen" are higher than
  the chance that a given person, a given night, would see one; the ranking (area under the curve) suffers less from it than the calibration.
- Most reports come from North America (the magnetic latitude makes them comparable with Europe; Europe is still scored alone above).
- A report may be an aurora seen on a phone photo, not with the eye; the form does not always say.
- The test years are those of a solar maximum: nights of strong storms are over-represented.

## 5. Reproduce

```
python tools/measure_aurora.py fetch
python tools/measure_aurora.py prepare
python tools/measure_aurora.py fit
python tools/measure_aurora.py score --markdown --json docs/measurements/aurora-2026-10-results.json
```

The two downloads (8 MB and 17 MB) go to the temporary folder; nothing from them is copied into the repository but the figures above.
